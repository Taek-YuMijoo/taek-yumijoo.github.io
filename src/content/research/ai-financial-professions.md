Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT have been in wide use since late 2022, but employment statistics do not yet show their effect on work. This study, a chapter in a Korea Labor Institute (KLI) report on AI, labor, productivity and inequality, takes finance as its case. Finance is already highly computerized in Korea and is often ranked among the industries most exposed to automation, yet earlier technologies such as ATMs did not reduce the number of bank tellers. Research on AI in credit scoring also points in opposite directions: some studies find wider access to credit, others find that biased data and model design deepen inequality.

The chapter asks how finance workers use AI at work now, how they expect it to change their tasks and the make-up of the workforce, and what new inequalities they see coming. It also asks whether generative AI is likely to replace professional jobs, as many forecasts assume.

The authors interviewed 12 people, one at a time, between July and September 2025 (four per month). Participants were recruited by snowball sampling, starting from finance workers in the research team's own networks, and received a small payment. They were not limited to banking, securities or fintech. Table 6-1 lists them: a data scientist at a bond-pricing firm, an investment reviewer at a life insurer, a system trader, a derivatives valuation specialist and an analyst at securities firms, developers at a crypto-asset trading platform and a bank, an AI quant researcher at a fintech start-up, an alternative-asset valuer, a venture capital investment reviewer, a fund manager at an asset manager, and a police officer on a phishing-crime unit. Nine were in their 30s and three in their 40s. Interviews were semi-structured and lasted about an hour. They moved from current AI use, to expectations for the future, to changes in staffing and job content, and ended with how AI skills might widen inequality. Recordings were transcribed with Naver Clova, and the authors extracted and compared statements by topic.

The findings fall into five groups.

- **Current use.** The most common use was gathering, translating and summarizing information, including foreign-language reports and emails to overseas vendors; one interviewee said expert analyst reports matter less now that a language model can produce analysis on demand. IT staff used AI to write SQL and code. Firms differed in attitude: some offered tuned in-house tools, others restricted AI because of data-leak concerns. Several interviewees said machine-learning models have not been shown to beat conventional quant models, although investment continues in the hope that one will.
- **Expected change.** Interviewees expected AI agents and personalized recommendations to take over parts of customer service. They said hallucinations still prevent full automation of report writing and tax or legal review; if accuracy improves, research, report writing and legal review would change substantially.
- **Staffing.** Simple, repetitive jobs (system trading, bond valuation, branch counter service) were seen as most replaceable. Interviewees said tacit knowledge, accumulated judgment and accountability for final outputs stay with people. A VC investment reviewer said the firm had stopped hiring interns because AI returns research faster; a team lead said he would hire one person and spend the rest on AI instead of five juniors.
- **Job content.** Opinions were split. One venture capital interviewee estimated the job had moved from roughly half office work and half sales to about 20 to 80, as AI absorbed research. Others saw little change so far, citing no clear effect on revenue and the difficulty of putting confidential client data into AI tools.
- **Inequality.** Interviewees pointed to a widening gap between junior and senior staff, since entry-level tasks are the ones automated; to a shift toward interpersonal skills that are not captured by quantitative performance metrics; to a shortage of candidates who know both finance and AI, with employers favoring master's and doctoral degrees; and to firm size, since data subscriptions cost one interviewee's team roughly 100 to 300 million won a year, which favors large firms.

The authors conclude that AI in finance follows the general pattern of automating simple, repetitive work, shaped by the particular features of the industry. They stress the human role of checking AI output, giving feedback and taking responsibility for results, and they ask whether junior staff and graduates can fairly gain the experience and credentials that the new skill mix requires. They note that the study is exploratory, that actual developments may differ, and that follow-up research should track them.

My role was research assistant in the Computational Analysis of Technology and Society Lab at KAIST (supervisor: Prof. Lanu Kim), from May to October 2025. I conducted qualitative interviews with financial professionals about their experience with AI tools. The chapter does not name its individual authors or interviewers. The study is relevant to my interest in AI and labor because it records how workers themselves describe changes in tasks, hiring and skill requirements while those changes are still under way, and because it links the task-level changes to inequality between juniors and seniors, between degree levels, and between large and small firms.
