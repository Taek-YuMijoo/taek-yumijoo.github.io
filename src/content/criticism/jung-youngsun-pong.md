The review looks at the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art's exhibition of the landscape architect Jung Youngsun, the museum's first landscape architecture show. I start from a loose definition of life as whatever is born and dies. That makes every living thing a process, and a process needs time. An exhibition that presents a garden has to show what time the garden belongs to. Otherwise it gives only a taxidermied specimen or, at best, a picturesque panorama.

I read the exhibition as that failure. Its photographs, planting plans and hand sketches work as separate images. Many drawings are unreadable behind reflective glass, and the seasonal photographs of the gardens Moheon and Joanri show different spots from different angles, so a visitor cannot tell one garden changing over time. I compare it with the Norman Foster exhibition at the Seoul Museum of Art, where models and drawings explained why the Apple headquarters looks as it does, and with time-based graphics such as plant palettes that landscape architects already use to show colour through the seasons.

The cause, as I see it, is a curatorial desire for the picturesque. Augustin Berque describes landscape as a concept that separates humans from nature, and Arnold Berleant proposes a participatory landscape in place of the panoramic one. The brochure speaks of life from microbes to the cosmos and quotes Jung's phrase that a landscape architect is a connector, yet the exhibition does not follow it. The garden in the exhibition yard was locked on three of my four visits. I end by separating the work of the garden from the work of the museum, and by saying that the art world should answer for an exhibition like this one.
