Every conversation rests on some phenomenon it refers to. Face to face, that phenomenon is usually available to everyone at once. Two people playing rock-paper-scissors both see who won. Move the same game into a chat room and the ground disappears: if one player types a second late, nothing in the text shows whether he chose his hand in advance or changed it after seeing the other's. Online talk about offline events cannot present the situation it rests on.

On social media and other online agoras that ask for no identity, this plays out daily. Someone posts a strange experience and other users demand evidence that it is not invented. The demand grows sharper on polarized topics such as politics and gender, where undermining the other side's claim becomes the main goal. I find this odd, because missing information is built into the medium. No platform can hold everything a person has lived through, and no one can fully verify how rigorous a piece of evidence is or whether it was faked. Opinion manipulation with carefully made evidence is already happening almost everywhere.

AI deepens the problem. Until recently one could at least trust that the other party was a person, even a paid one. Now accounts run by AI address people in many languages on X, and generated comments multiply on YouTube. They will get harder to tell apart, and what they rest on is scraped online data and the one-sided intent of whoever runs the bot. So I argue that participants should shift from demanding proof of fact to estimating how likely it is that what is said could happen. That estimate makes it unnecessary to know whether the other party is a human or an AI. It also leaves each user responsible for the credibility of the conversation, which is a loss, and a kind of freedom, since each person judges their own world. It will depend on intuition, media literacy and statistical judgment, and the education we plan for the agora should teach them.
