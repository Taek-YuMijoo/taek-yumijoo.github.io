Criticism comes after viewing, and viewing depends on a finished exhibition. If a show does not give visitors a stable place to look, I argue, it is not finished, and it makes viewing and criticism impossible. The essay takes up that situation. Museums put a great deal of effort into reaching "more" visitors, so I ask whether their universal design and barrier-free measures work in practice. An idea that is not carried out is only a gesture. I treat the visitor as a user and read the exhibition through design criticism and the language of user experience.

My case is the Korea Artist Prize 2023 at the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, Seoul. In the corridor at the entrance, the interview videos of the shortlisted artists sit behind translucent cells, in front of a large wall of English lettering that gives the interviewees' names. The lettering is hard to read, it is hard to see from where the monitors stand, and two of the interviews are placed so that viewers turn their backs to it. A visitor can hardly tell who is speaking, so the display assumes viewers already know every artist's face.

The problems continue inside. In Gala Porras-Kim's room, emails that form part of the work are printed too small to read without standing very close. In Jeon Soyoung's room, captions are printed on glossy film that reflects the strong lighting, so they stay unreadable from any angle. I do not accept calling these small matters unrelated to the art. If a good exhibition gives more people a fuller viewing, the 2024 prize has room to improve.
