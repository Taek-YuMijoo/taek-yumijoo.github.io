I wrote this essay in October 2020 against the wave of trot variety shows that followed Miss Trot. I have no complaint about the first season, which gave exposure to singers who had gone unnoticed. The trouble came after its success. The format became a worse version of Show Me the Money, in which young musicians stake their bodies on a gamble, sing music they did not grow up with, and face a panel from a much older generation.

My claim is that the main subject of these shows is youth, and trot is a minor presence. Little of the music is trot. Lim Young-woong's most-viewed videos on YouTube are covers of folk and ballad songs, and the contestants sing folk, ballad and rock numbers with almost no change of genre. The audience does not mind. The top seven of Mr. Trot averaged 28 years old, and the oldest was 43. I also dispute a Newsis report that reads concert bookers in their twenties as proof that trot is a genre for all ages. I read it as children booking tickets online for their parents.

The generation that holds the money and the power has lost its authority, and these shows let it believe it can win that authority back. I find the result grotesque when a team of young girls on Voice Trot sings a song about burying one's past, or when a child is made to sing in a way that can damage her voice. I do not want the young, who are still talked over, to be turned into performers for this audience. I end with Na Hoon-a, who staged an untact concert during the pandemic, appeared without a fee, and broadcast it over the Chuseok holiday. I take him as an older person who looks at his own age directly.
